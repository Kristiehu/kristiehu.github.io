A fibre run is recorded as an A end, a Z end, and the structures spliced between them,
in order. Usually that order is right. Sometimes two stops are the wrong way round, or
one points at a structure on the other side of the city because the wrong name got
picked from a dropdown. Checking by hand means opening the cable, pulling up the map and
following the points one by one (fine for one, impossible for a few thousand).

This reads every cable's recorded order against where the structures actually sit, and
returns the ones that are hard to explain, worst first.

**Nothing below is real data.** The cable IDs, the structure names, the coordinates, the
streets under them and the recorded orders are all generated by a script from a single
seed. The checks are the ones we run; what they are running on is invented.

Pick a cable and its recorded order is drawn on the map, numbered. Red marks the
positions the check wants a human to look at, and the panel says what to do about each
one.

## What it flags

It never calls a cable correct. It knows where the structures sit and what order the
record claims, and nothing about which streets the cable may follow, so all it can do is
spot an order that takes explaining:

- the route crosses itself
- a point sits behind one listed ahead of it
- the run turns straight back the way it came
- the same structure appears twice
- one span is far longer than the rest

One of those on its own means little (a lateral down a dead end trips two and is
perfectly correct). Three at once is worth ten minutes. The goal is not to replace human 
validation, but to prioritize which records deserve human attention.
